\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

\subsection{Setup}

\paragraph{Decode cost.} We measure single-core median decode latency for 4\,KB packets on an
AWS c7i.2xlarge instance (Intel Xeon Platinum 8488C, Sapphire Rapids, AVX-512 and GFNI,
Ubuntu~24.04), with Intel ISA-L~v2.31 and cm256. We repeat every measurement on an Intel Core
i9-14900HX laptop as a cross-check. All decoders receive identical data, loss patterns and survivor sets, and every
decode is verified byte-for-byte. We compare three decoders:
\begin{itemize}
  \item ISA-L's documented decode path, which inverts the $k\times k$ survivor matrix for every loss pattern;
  \item cm256, which eliminates the received data and solves an $m'\times m'$ Cauchy system by LDU decomposition;
  \item erasure-count decoding~\cite{li2005patent} on ISA-L's GFNI kernel.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Network model.} A discrete-event simulator sends one RDMA message, or 64 messages in parallel
for a collective step, over a 100\,Gbps link with 4\,KB MTU. One-way delay is RTT/2 and losses are
Bernoulli. It compares:
\begin{itemize}
  \item go-back-N (GBN), as in commodity RoCE NICs;
  \item IRN-style selective repeat (SR)~\cite{irn};
  \item SR with a tail-loss probe after $2\times$RTT (SR+TLP);
  \item systematic RS($n,k$) FEC with a selective-repeat fallback.
\end{itemize}
FEC decode times are the measured medians above, queued on one core. The retransmission timeout is
100\,\textmu s for every scheme. For each point we report the best of the codes
RS(17,16), RS(18,16), RS(20,16), RS(34,32) and RS(36,32). The simulator reproduces 17 hand-derived
completion times exactly, and all schemes see the same random loss draws. We report the
99th-percentile completion time over 5{,}000 messages (1{,}000 collective steps).

\subsection{Decode cost}

On the server, ISA-L takes about 21\,\textmu s for a cold decode at $k{=}16$ and about
160\,\textmu s at $k{=}32$, almost all of it in matrix inversion; its packet work takes
0.8--3.9\,\textmu s. Erasure-count decoding on the same kernel removes almost all of the inversion
cost. With one or two lost packets and $k\ge16$ it decodes 17--91$\times$ faster than ISA-L, and
between 23\% slower and 15\% faster than ISA-L with a pre-cached decode matrix. Against cm256 it is
1.0--1.5$\times$ faster when one packet is lost ($k\ge16$) and 2.1--2.6$\times$ faster when two are
lost. That gain comes from the GFNI kernel, not the decoding algorithm, since both decoders solve
the same $m'\times m'$ system. The laptop shows the same pattern with a smaller gap: ISA-L's scalar
inversion is about 2--3$\times$ faster there, so erasure-count decoding is 11--43$\times$ faster
than ISA-L instead of 17--91$\times$.

\paragraph{Decode cost decides the winner.} Table~\ref{tab:collective} shows a 64-flow collective
step at $10^{-3}$ loss. With ISA-L's $k\times k$ decoder, FEC \emph{loses} to selective repeat with a
tail-loss probe at every RTT up to 10\,\textmu s, by 1.5--3.0$\times$ (48.0 vs.\ 15.8\,\textmu s at
RTT 2\,\textmu s). The same FEC scheme with an erasure-count decoder \emph{wins} at every RTT, by
1.2--3.0$\times$, and comes within 5--8\% of an FEC scheme with free decoding. Neither the code nor
the loss process changes between these runs, only the decoder. The same reversal appears at lower
loss: at $10^{-4}$ and RTT 5\,\textmu s, erasure-count FEC takes 14.5\,\textmu s, retransmission
18.3\,\textmu s, and FEC with ISA-L 34.4\,\textmu s. With the laptop's faster ISA-L inversion the
ISA-L column drops to 27.6--39.5\,\textmu s at $10^{-3}$ and FEC with ISA-L still loses at RTTs of 2
and 5\,\textmu s.

\paragraph{Why retransmission loses the tail.} A lost final packet produces no out-of-order arrival
to trigger a NAK, so plain SR and GBN wait for the timeout. Their p99 for a 64-flow step jumps from
18 to 118\,\textmu s between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$ loss. A tail-loss probe recovers most of this,
but not when the probe or several final packets are lost. At $10^{-2}$ loss SR+TLP again reaches
118\,\textmu s, while erasure-count FEC stays at 16.7\,\textmu s and FEC with ISA-L at 50.3\,\textmu s.

\paragraph{When FEC does not pay.} Parity costs bandwidth: 6.25\% for RS(17,16) and RS(34,32).
Below about $10^{-4}$ loss, or for single messages of 2\,MB and up, this overhead outweighs the
recovery benefit. For example, a single 2\,MB message at $10^{-3}$ loss and RTT 5\,\textmu s
completes in 173\,\textmu s with SR and in 181\,\textmu s with FEC. Figure~\ref{fig:ratio} maps the
boundary. FEC with an erasure-count decoder wins for small messages at loss rates of $10^{-3}$ and
above, and the region where it wins grows with RTT. ISA-L decoding shrinks that region to almost
nothing at RTTs of 5\,\textmu s or less.

\paragraph{How much parity.} Figure~\ref{fig:overhead} plots tail latency against parity overhead.
At $10^{-4}$--$10^{-3}$ loss the 6.25\% codes are best: RS(17,16) completes a 64-flow step of
128\,KB in 14.5\,\textmu s, against 23.3\,\textmu s for the best retransmission. At $10^{-2}$ loss
they no longer carry enough parity. Too many codewords lose more than one packet, fall back to
retransmission and hit the timeout (118.6\,\textmu s). RS(20,16), with 25\% overhead, completes in
16.7\,\textmu s. The right amount of parity therefore grows with the loss rate. For single messages
of 2\,MB no amount of parity beats retransmission below 1\% loss.

\paragraph{Bursty loss calibrated to production measurements.} Losses in datacenters are bursty.
Ghabashneh et al.~\cite{ghabashneh2022} measured Meta racks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item bursts last a median of 2\,ms (90th percentile 8\,ms);
  \item they occur 7.5--39.8 times per second;
  \item 0.4--5\% of them drop packets.
\end{itemize}
We convert these numbers into a two-state burst model that advances in wall-clock time, with
lossy bursts of mean 2.9\,ms occupying $2.2\times10^{-4}$ (typical rack), $1.15\times10^{-3}$
(busy) or $5.8\times10^{-3}$ (contended) of the time. The loss rate inside a burst is not
published, so we sweep it. The same reversal appears (Figure~\ref{fig:bursts}). For a 64-flow step
of 128\,KB in a busy rack with 10\% loss inside bursts, the best retransmission takes
25.0\,\textmu s, FEC with ISA-L 51.7\,\textmu s, and FEC with erasure-count decoding
17.1\,\textmu s. In a contended rack with 1\% in-burst loss the figures are 18.3, 34.4 and
14.5\,\textmu s. At 30\% in-burst loss every scheme degrades, because no code we evaluate carries
enough parity for a multi-millisecond burst.

\paragraph{Which erasure-count decoder matters little.} cm256 and erasure-count decoding on ISA-L's
kernel differ by at most 2.2\,\textmu s at the 99th percentile in the collective configurations above
(3.7\,\textmu s across the full laptop sweep), even though cm256 decodes up to 2.6$\times$ slower. Once decoding costs about
1\,\textmu s, RTT and serialisation dominate. The decisive difference is $k\times k$ inversion versus
erasure-count decoding, not which erasure-count implementation is used.

\subsection{Limitations}
Our model has no congestion control, PFC or NIC pipeline. The flows in a collective step are
independent and do not share a bottleneck, the sender is not window-limited, and acknowledgements
are never lost. Decode costs are single-core medians from one cloud server and one laptop, without
frequency pinning (EC2 guests do not expose it). In the bursty model, bursts are independent
across flows, whereas a real incast burst hits every flow to the same receiver at once, and the
in-burst loss rate is swept rather than measured. Commodity RoCE timeouts are often far above
100\,\textmu s, which would favour FEC and SR+TLP further.
